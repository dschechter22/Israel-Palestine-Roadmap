      Common comparisons

      A "two-state solution" means an independent Palestine next to Israel. It has been proposed, in different shapes, for almost a century. Every plan has to answer the same four questions: where the borders go (and what happens to settlements), who gets Jerusalem, what happens to Palestinian refugees and their descendants, and how each side stays safe.

      Most of these offers were never put on paper, so the maps below are rough sketches based on what participants and reporters described. They show the idea, not the exact lines. The table gives the quick version; scroll down for each plan in detail.

      Other ideas people propose

      One state. One country for everyone between the river and the sea, with equal rights. Some Palestinians and some Israeli and international activists support it. Most Israelis reject it because Jews would lose their majority, and critics on all sides doubt two peoples in conflict could share one government.

      Confederation. Two states with open borders and some shared institutions, sometimes called "two states, one homeland." Settlers could stay in Palestine as residents, and Palestinians could live in Israel. It's a small but growing proposal from Israeli and Palestinian groups.

      Managing the status quo. No final deal, with Israel keeping security control and Palestinians having limited self-rule. Critics on the left call this permanent occupation. Critics on the right want formal annexation of all or part of the West Bank instead.

      Major events, in order

      Tap any event for who carried it out, who was harmed, and sources. Here the marker color shows who carried out the act, not who was harmed. Solid markers are events counted mainly in deaths; hollow markers are raids, arrests, demolitions and expulsions that killed few or no one but affected many. Bigger markers mean more people killed or affected.

      This roadmap walks through the Israeli-Palestinian conflict from the late 1800s to November 2026. Each stop on the timeline is a period. The map shows who controlled what, the panel under it shows who was fighting whom, and the text tells the story. "Go deeper" links at each stop cover the background and related questions that help the period make sense.

      How it handles disagreement

      Where historians or the two sides disagree, the stop has a section called "Where people disagree" that lays out each side's account, says who holds it, and lists sources for each. Loaded words like "occupation," "apartheid," "genocide" and "terrorism" are credited to whoever uses them. Where a court or a UN body used the word, the page says so.

      Sources

      Each source says what kind it is (an original document, a government or UN record, a court record, a reference book, a historian, a human rights group, or news) and, if it's known for a point of view, what that is. Original documents and official records are used wherever they exist. Links go to the source; books don't have links. Not every sentence was checked against its source while building this, so treat the lists as places to verify, especially for numbers that matter to you.

      Numbers corrected while building this

      • First Intifada deaths: about 1,200 to 1,400 Palestinians and 170 to 185 Israelis (B'Tselem, depending on what's counted).
      • Samu raid, 1966: the mine killed three Israeli Border Police. Houses destroyed: 40 by Israel's count, 125 by the UN's. 16 Jordanian soldiers and 3 civilians were killed.
      • Gaza: about 67,200 dead on the day of the October 2025 ceasefire by the Health Ministry's count, and about 74,400 by October 2026. Israel's army says it killed over 24,000 fighters.
      • About 157 of 193 UN members recognize Palestine.
      • Israeli security personnel killed since October 7, 2023: 1,152 as of October 2025 (Israel's Defense Ministry).
      • Lebanon, 1982: Lebanese police counted 19,085 killed through August 31, 1982; the newspaper An-Nahar counted 17,825.
      • Kafr Qasim, 1956: 48 killed, or 49 counting an unborn child.

      About the maps

      The maps are hand-drawn pixel sketches, good for seeing big changes, not for exact lines. Plans that were never put on paper are drawn from participants' descriptions.

      Books, by point of view

      • Ian Black, Enemies and Neighbours. Tells both stories side by side. A good place to start.
      • Rashid Khalidi, The Hundred Years' War on Palestine. Palestinian point of view.
      • Benny Morris, Righteous Victims. Israeli historian whose archive research on 1948 challenged Israel's founding story, though his own politics later moved right.
      • Tom Segev, One Palestine, Complete. The British years, by an Israeli writer.
      • Avi Shlaim, The Iron Wall. Israel and the Arab world after 1948, from a critical Israeli-British historian.
      • Anita Shapira, Israel: A History, and Daniel Gordis, Israel: A Concise History. Mainstream Israeli and Israel-sympathetic views. Neither claims Israel's record is clean.
      • Steer clear of Joan Peters, From Time Immemorial, which historians have largely discredited.
